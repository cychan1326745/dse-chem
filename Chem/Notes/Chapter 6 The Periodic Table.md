# Chapter 6 The Periodic Table
## 6.1 Classifying elements into ==metals, non‑metals and semi‑metals==

### Table 6.1 Some typical differences in physical properties of metals and non-metals
| Property                                         | Metals                                      | Non-metals                                                                                              |
| ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Physical state at room temperature and pressure  | ==solids== (except mercury)                 | ==gases== or ==solids== (except bromine)                                                                |
| Appearance                                       | ==shiny==                                   | usually ==dull== in appearance                                                                          |
| Melting point and boiling point                  | usually ==high==                            | usually ==low== (some non-metals like diamond and graphite have high melting points and boiling points) |
| Hardness and strength                            | usually ==hard and strong===                | usually ==brittle== (broken easily when a force is applied)                                             |
| Malleability and ductility                       | ==malleable== and ==ductile==               | ==not malleable== and ==not ductile== when solid                                                        |
| Density                                          | usually ==high==                            | ==low==                                                                                                 |
| Thermal conductivity and electrical conductivity | ==good conductors of heat and electricity== | ==poor conductors of heat and non-conductors of electricity== (**except graphite**)                     |

There are **exceptions** to the properties mentioned above. For example:
‑ <u>*Sodium*</u> is a metal but is so soft that it can be easily cut with a knife. Besides, it has a low melting point (below $100^\circ\text{C}$) and has a low density, so it floats on water.
‑ <u>*Graphite*</u> (a form of carbon) is a non‑metal but it is an electrical conductor. It is also shiny and has a very high melting point ($3730^\circ\text{C}$).

### Classifying an element according to its physical properties
To decide whether a certain element is a metal or non‑metal, we first consider the physical state of the element at room temperature and pressure.

‑ Non‑metals include all of the gaseous elements, one liquid element (bromine) and a few solid elements.
‑ Metals include most of the solid elements and one liquid element (mercury).
![[Ch6_001.png]]

### Semi‑metals (metalloids, e.g. Si, Ge, As)
Properties intermediate between metals and non‑metals (have some properties similar to those of metals and some properties similar to those of non‑metals.). Silicon is a typical semiconductor, used in computer chips.

### DSE key point
Classification depends on chemical behaviour (electron‑losing/gaining tendency) rather than only physical properties.

## 6.2 Development of the Periodic Table
Modern Periodic Table: Arranged by **atomic (proton) number**.
![[Ch6_002.png]]
### Periods
A horizontal row of elements in the Periodic Table is called a **period**. Each period has a number: from Period 1 to Period 7. Period 1 contains only two elements — hydrogen and helium. Period 2 and Period 3 each contain eight elements. Other periods contain more than eight elements.

Period 1 elements have ==one occupied electron shell== in each of their atoms. Period 2 elements have ==two occupied electron shells== in each of their atoms , and so on.

> **Key point**
> Period number of an element
> = ==number of occupied electron shells== in an atom of that element

### Groups
A vertical column of elements in the Periodic Table is called a **group**. There are altogether eight main groups. Each group has a number (I, II, III, IV, V, VI, VII or 0).

Group I elements have ==one outermost shell electron== in each of their atoms (Table 6.2 on p.42). Group II elements have ==two outermost shell electrons== in each of their atoms, and so on.

> **Key point**
> Group number of an element = ==number of electrons in the outermost shell== in an atom of that element.

|Group number|Group name|
|---|---|
|Group I|Alkali metals|
|Group II|Alkaline earth metals|
|Group VII|Halogens|
|Group 0|Noble gases|

## 6.3 Patterns in the Periodic Table

### Periodic trends (across a Period, from left to right)
1. Elements change from metal → semi‑metal → non‑metal.
### Group trends (down a group)
1. For metal groups (Group I, II): Metal reactivity increases down the group (easier to lose valence electrons).
2. For non‑metal group (Group VII): Non‑metal reactivity decreases down the group (harder to gain electrons).

---

## 6.4 Group I: The alkali metals (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs)
### Common structure feature
==one outermost shell electron== in the atoms; tend to lose one electron to form +1 ions ($M^+$).

### Similarities of Group I elements
1. All are soft metals.
2. All have low densities.
3. All are reactive metals.
4. All react with water to form an alkaline solution. Hydrogen is given off.
5. All react with non‑metals to form ionic compounds.

### Typical reactions (DSE required)
1. Reaction with water:
$2M(s)+2H_2O(l)\rightarrow 2MOH(aq)+H_2(g)$
Reaction becomes more vigorous down the group. Sodium reacts vigorously; potassium ignites spontaneously in water.
2. Reaction with oxygen: Form metal oxide, e.g. $4Na+O_2\rightarrow 2Na_2O$

### Important notes
‑Alkali metals are stored under oil/paraffin to avoid contact with air and water.
‑All Group I compounds are soluble in water.
-Although all alkali metals are reactive, they differ in reactivities. For example, the reaction of alkali metals with water becomes more vigorous when moving down the group.(Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs)
---

## 6.5 Group II: The alkaline earth metals (Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba)
### Structure feature
==two outermost shell electrons==; lose 2 electrons to form +2 ions ($M^{2+}$).

### Similarities of Group II elements
1. All have low densities.
2. All are less reactive than alkali metals.
3. All, except beryllium, react with water less vigorously than Group I elements.
4. All react readily with dilute hydrochloric acid to form hydrogen.
5. All (with some exceptions e.g. beryllium) react with non‑metals to form ionic compounds.

> **Key point**
> The reaction of alkaline earth metals with water becomes more vigorous when moving down the group.

---

## 6.6 Group VII: The halogens ($F_2,Cl_2,Br_2,I_2$)
### Structure feature
 ==7 outermost shell electrons==; tend to gain one electron to form −1 ions ($X^‑$). Exist as diatomic molecules $X_2$.

### Physical state change down the group (room temperature):
Fluorine (gas) → Chlorine (gas) → Bromine (liquid) → Iodine (solid); melting/boiling points increase.

### Similarities of Group VII elements
1. All have colours.
2. All react with metals to form ionic compounds.
3. All react with non‑metals to form covalent compounds.

> **Key point**
> Reactivity of Group VII elements decreases down the group.
---

## 6.7 Group 0: The noble gases (He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe)
### Structure feature
Full‑filled outermost electron shell (2 electrons for He, 8 electrons for others). Stable electron arrangement, very unreactive, chemically inert.

### Properties
1. Monatomic gases, colourless at room temperature
2. Low melting/boiling points, increase down the group (increasing relative atomic mass, stronger van der Waals’ force)
3. No tendency to gain or lose electrons, rarely form compounds under normal conditions.

### Uses
‑Helium: Balloons (low density, unreactive)
‑Neon: Neon lighting
‑Argon: Inert atmosphere for welding, light bulbs.

> **Key point**
> Octet rule states that all atoms tend to attain the stable electronic arrangement of a noble gas (in most cases an ‘octet’).

## 6.8 Predicting the properties of an unfamiliar element in a group of the Periodic Table
### Basic prediction rules

### DSE exam strategy
‑Use valence electron number to judge chemical similarity; use atomic size trend to judge reactivity change.

#### **Example 6.4 Predicting the properties of an unfamiliar element in Group I**
> The table below gives some information about the six elements in Group I of the Periodic Table.

| Element   | Atomic number | Melting point /℃ | Reaction with water                        |
| --------- | ------------- | ---------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Lithium   | 3             | 181              | Reacts moderately                          |
| Sodium    | 11            | 98               | Reacts rapidly                             |
| Potassium | 19            | 63               | Reacts vigorously                          |
| Rubidium  | 37            | 40               | Reacts explosively                         |
| Caesium   | 55            | 28               | Reacts even more explosively than rubidium |
| Francium  | 87            | ?                | ?                                          |

> **(a) Would you expect the melting point of francium to be higher or lower than 28℃? Explain your answer.**
> > ANS: The melting point of francium should be lower than 28℃ because the melting point of the elements decreases down the group.
>
> **(b) Sodium is a silvery solid, which can be cut easily with a knife. Predict the appearance of francium. Do you think francium can be cut easily with a knife?**
> > ANS: Francium is a silvery solid. It can be cut easily with a knife.
>
> **(c) Caesium reacts with water explosively. Predict the observation when francium is added to water. Explain your answer.**
> > ANS: Francium reacts with water even more explosively. The reactivity of Group I elements increases down the group.

## DSE Common Misconceptions
1. ❌ Group I reactivity decreases down the group
✅ Increases; outer electron further from nucleus, easier to lose.
2. ❌ Group VII reactivity increases down the group
✅ Decreases; larger atom harder to gain an extra electron.
3. ❌ Mendeleev arranged elements by proton number
✅ Mendeleev used relative atomic mass; modern table uses atomic (proton) number.
4. ❌ Noble gases form ions easily
✅ Full outer electron shell, very stable, rarely form ions or compounds.
5. ❌ Across a period atomic radius increases
✅ Decreases due to increasing nuclear attraction on outer electrons.
