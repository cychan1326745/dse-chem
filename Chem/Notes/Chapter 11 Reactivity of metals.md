# Chapter 11 Reactivity of metals
## 11.1 Reactions of metals with oxygen
Most metals react with oxygen in the air to form metal oxides. Heat is needed to start these reactions.

General word equation: Metal + Oxygen → Metal oxide

### Appearance of metals and storage methods
All metals appear shiny when they are freshly cut. However, many metals soon become dull when exposed to air. This is mainly due to the reaction of metals with oxygen (in the air), forming an oxide layer on the metal surface.

To prevent them from reacting with oxygen (in the air):
- Very reactive metals such as potassium and sodium are stored under paraffin oil.
- Calcium, which is quite reactive, is kept in an airtight container.

### Summary table of metals reacting with oxygen (in air)
- **Potassium (K)**
  Condition: gentle heating
  Observation: Burns vigorously with a lilac flame to produce a white powder.
  Word equation: potassium + oxygen → potassium oxide
  Chemical equation: $$4K(s)+O_2(g)\xrightarrow{\Delta}2K_2O(s)$$

- **Sodium (Na)**
  Condition: gentle heating
  Observation: Burns vigorously with a golden yellow flame to produce a white powder.
  Word equation: sodium + oxygen → sodium oxide
  Chemical equation: $$4Na(s)+O_2(g)\xrightarrow{\Delta}2Na_2O(s)$$

- **Calcium (Ca)**
  Condition: strong heating
  Observation: Burns quite vigorously with a brick-red flame to produce a white powder.
  Word equation: calcium + oxygen → calcium oxide
  Chemical equation: $$2Ca(s)+O_2(g)\xrightarrow{\Delta}2CaO(s)$$

- **Magnesium (Mg)**
  Condition: strong heating
  Observation: Burns with a very bright white flame to produce a white powder.
  Word equation: magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide
  Chemical equation: $$2Mg(s)+O_2(g)\xrightarrow{\text{burn}}2MgO(s)$$

- **Aluminium (Al)**
  Condition: strong heating
  Observation: Aluminium powder burns to give out much heat; a white powder forms.
  Word equation: aluminium + oxygen → aluminium oxide
  Chemical equation: $$4Al(s)+3O_2(g)\xrightarrow{\Delta}2Al_2O_3(s)$$

- **Zinc (Zn)**
  Condition: strong heating
  Observation: Zinc powder burns to give out some heat; a powder (yellow when hot, white when cold) forms.
  Word equation: zinc + oxygen → zinc oxide
  Chemical equation: $$2Zn(s)+O_2(g)\xrightarrow{\Delta}2ZnO(s)$$

- **Iron (Fe)**
  Condition: strong heating
  Observation: Iron powder burns with yellow sparks to produce a black solid.
  Word equation: iron + oxygen → iron(II,III) oxide
  Chemical equation: $$3Fe(s)+2O_2(g)\xrightarrow{\Delta}Fe_3O_4(s)$$

- **Lead (Pb)**
  Condition: strong heating
  Observation: It melts; a powder (orange when hot, yellow when cold) is seen on the surface.
  Word equation: lead + oxygen → lead(II) oxide
  Chemical equation: $$2Pb(s)+O_2(g)\xrightarrow{\Delta}2PbO(s)$$

- **Copper (Cu)**
  Condition: very strong heating
  Observation: Its surface turns black.
  Word equation: copper + oxygen → copper(II) oxide
  Chemical equation: $$2Cu(s)+O_2(g)\xrightarrow{\Delta}2CuO(s)$$

- **Mercury (Hg)**
  Condition: very strong heating
  Observation: A red powder forms on the surface.
  Word equation: mercury + oxygen → mercury(II) oxide
  Chemical equation: $$2Hg(l)+O_2(g)\xrightarrow{\Delta}2HgO(s)$$

- **Silver (Ag)**
  Condition: —
  Observation: No observable change even on very strong heating.
  Word equation: No reaction

- **Platinum (Pt)**
  Condition: —
  Observation: No observable change even on very strong heating.
  Word equation: No reaction

- **Gold (Au)**
  Condition: —
  Observation: No observable change even on very strong heating.
  Word equation: No reaction

## 11.2 Reactions of metals with water
General word equation: Metal + Water → Metal hydroxide + Hydrogen

### Metals reacting with cold water
| Metal | Observations |
| ---- | ---- |
| Potassium (K) | • melts to form a silvery ball<br>• moves about very quickly on the water surface with a hissing sound<br>• burns with a lilac flame |
| Sodium (Na) | • melts to form a silvery ball<br>• moves about quickly on the water surface<br>• burns with a golden yellow flame |
| Calcium (Ca) | • does not burn, but sinks to the bottom of water<br>• colourless gas bubbles form at a moderate rate |

Sodium
Word equation: Sodium + Water → Sodium hydroxide + Hydrogen
Chemical equation:
$$2Na(s) + 2H_2O(l) \rightarrow 2NaOH(aq) + H_2(g)\uparrow$$

Calcium
Word equation: Calcium + Water → Calcium hydroxide + Hydrogen
Chemical equation:
$$Ca(s) + 2H_2O(l) \rightarrow Ca(OH)_2(aq) + H_2(g)\uparrow$$

### Metals reacting with steam only
Magnesium
Word equation: Magnesium + Steam → Magnesium oxide + Hydrogen
Chemical equation:
$$Mg(s) + H_2O(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta} MgO(s) + H_2(g)$$

Metals below Mg (Zn, Fe): React only with steam; Metals below Pb: No reaction with water / steam.

## 11.3 Reactions of metals with dilute hydrochloric acid & dilute sulphuric acid
General word equation: Metal + Dilute acid → Soluble salt + Hydrogen gas
**Only metals above hydrogen in reactivity series can react.**

1. Zinc + dilute hydrochloric acid
Word equation: Zinc + Dilute hydrochloric acid → Zinc chloride + Hydrogen
Chemical equation:
$$Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) \rightarrow ZnCl_2(aq) + H_2(g)\uparrow$$

2. Iron + dilute sulphuric acid
Word equation: Iron + Dilute sulphuric acid → Iron(II) sulphate + Hydrogen
Chemical equation:
$$Fe(s) + H_2SO_4(aq) \rightarrow FeSO_4(aq) + H_2(g)\uparrow$$

Test for hydrogen: Bring burning splint to gas; characteristic 'pop' sound.
Copper, silver, gold: No reaction with dilute acids.

## 11.4 Metal reactivity series and tendency to form positive ions
> **Key point**
> Metals react by losing electrons from their atoms to form positive ions.

### Reactivity and readiness to lose electrons
Reactivity of a metal depends on how readily its atoms lose electrons. The more readily the metal atoms lose electrons, the more reactive is the metal.
- The readiness of the metal atoms to lose electrons **decreases when moving across a period**.
  Example: across Period 3, reactivity of metals decreases in the order $\boldsymbol{Na > Mg > Al}$.
- The readiness of the metal atoms to lose electrons **increases when moving down a group**.
  Example:
  Group I: $\boldsymbol{Li < Na < K}$
  Group II: $\boldsymbol{Be < Mg < Ca}$

> **Learning tip**
> The electronic arrangements of K and Na atoms are 2,8,8,1 and 2,8,1 respectively. The outermost shell electron of K atom is farther from its nucleus than that of Na atom. Hence, the attraction between the outermost shell electron of K atom and its nucleus is weaker. This explains why K is more reactive than Na.

From the above discussion, the order of reactivity of some metals can be explained: $\boldsymbol{K > Na, Ca > Mg > Al...}$

> **Key point**
> A metal higher in the reactivity series is more reactive, and its atoms would lose electrons to form positive ions more readily.

Reactivity series (Most reactive → Least reactive):
$$K,\;Na,\;Ca,\;Mg,\;Al,\;Zn,\;Fe,\;Pb,\;(H),\;Cu,\;Ag,\;Pt,\;Au$$

Trend summary:
- High reactivity → Easily loses electrons → Strong reducing agent
- Low reactivity → Hard to lose electrons

## 11.5 Relate metal extraction method to reactivity series
1. **K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al (Very reactive)**
Cannot be reduced by carbon. Extracted by electrolysis of molten ores.

2. **Zn, Fe, Pb (Moderately reactive)**
Metal oxides can be reduced by carbon upon heating.
Example:
Word equation: Iron(III) oxide + Carbon $\xrightarrow{\Delta}$ Iron + Carbon dioxide
$$2Fe_2O_3(s) + 3C(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 4Fe(s) + 3CO_2(g)$$

3. **Cu, Hg (Low reactivity)**
Ores can be decomposed by heating alone (thermal decomposition).

4. **Ag, Pt, Au (Least reactive)**
Found as free native metal in nature; no chemical extraction required.

## 11.6 Writing chemical equations
Rules for balanced chemical equations:
1. Write correct chemical formulae for all reactants and products
2. Add state symbols: $(s)$ solid, $(l)$ liquid, $(g)$ gas, $(aq)$ aqueous solution
3. Balance the number of atoms of each element on both sides
4. Use $\uparrow$ for gaseous product, $\downarrow$ for precipitate
5. Indicate reaction conditions ($\Delta$ for heating, electric current etc.)

Word equation is a descriptive form; chemical equation uses formulae.

## 11.7 Displacement reactions of metals
Rule: A **more reactive metal** displaces a less reactive metal from its compound (oxide / aqueous salt solution).

### Type 1: Displacement from metal oxide
Example: Magnesium + Copper(II) oxide
Word equation: Magnesium + Copper(II) oxide $\xrightarrow{\Delta}$ Magnesium oxide + Copper
$$Mg(s) + CuO(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} MgO(s) + Cu(s)$$
Mg > Cu in reactivity series, reaction occurs.
Reverse reaction $Cu(s)+MgO(s)$: No reaction.

### Type 2: Displacement from aqueous salt solution
Example: Iron and copper(II) sulphate solution
Word equation: Iron + Copper(II) sulphate → Iron(II) sulphate + Copper
$$Fe(s) + CuSO_4(aq) \rightarrow FeSO_4(aq) + Cu(s)$$
Observation: Blue solution turns pale green; reddish-brown copper deposits.

$Cu(s) + FeSO_4(aq)$: No reaction.

## 11.8 Writing ionic equations
Steps to construct ionic equations:
1. Write balanced full chemical equation
2. Split soluble ionic compounds (aqueous) into free ions
3. Leave solids, liquids, gases in molecular form
4. Cancel spectator ions (ions appear on both sides)
5. Rewrite the remaining species to get ionic equation

Example: Reaction between zinc and dilute hydrochloric acid
Full equation:
$$Zn(s)+2HCl(aq)\rightarrow ZnCl_2(aq)+H_2(g)\uparrow$$
Ionic form:
$$Zn(s)+2H^+(aq)+2Cl^-(aq)\rightarrow Zn^{2+}(aq)+2Cl^-(aq)+H_2(g)\uparrow$$
Cancel spectator ion $Cl^-$
Final ionic equation:
$$Zn(s)+2H^+(aq)\rightarrow Zn^{2+}(aq)+H_2(g)\uparrow$$
